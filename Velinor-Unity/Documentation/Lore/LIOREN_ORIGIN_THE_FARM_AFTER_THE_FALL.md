# THE FARM AFTER THE FALL
*(Lore Narrative — paragraph form, action‑driven, novella‑compatible)*

When the Corelink system failed, it did not roar or shatter or burn. It simply broke the part of the human mind that understood the world. One moment Velhara's citizens were moving through glass corridors lit by soft blue panels; the next they were standing still, staring at interfaces they could no longer interpret, listening to alarms they could no longer decode. People stepped onto mag‑lev trains without understanding speed or direction. Some were thrown against walls when the trains accelerated. Others wandered into maintenance shafts, following lights that meant nothing anymore. Climate systems drifted out of balance. Air thinned. Heat rose. The glass city became a sealed oven full of people who had forgotten how to save themselves.

Someone found an emergency hatch. No one remembers who. The crowd surged toward it, driven by instinct rather than coordination. People were crushed in the rush; others were left behind in the glass sarcophagus as the temperature climbed. Those who escaped spilled into the open air, disoriented and terrified, confronted by a world still in the throes of climate collapse. The sky was a violent color. The wind carried dust hot enough to sting. The ground cracked underfoot. They had no plan, no direction, only hunger and the fading memory of how to survive.

That was when the agro‑research test farm saved them.

The farm had been built outside Velhara's enclosure, a sprawling field of engineered crops designed to withstand extreme heat, drought, nutrient‑poor soil, and rapid biome shifts. Before the collapse, it had been tended by agro‑engineers who studied how to grow food in a dying world. After the collapse, it became the only place where anything still grew. The escaping citizens stumbled into the fields, starving and confused, and began grabbing whatever they could carry: red amaranth heads, hardy lentil roots, millet clusters, teff analogs, buckwheat hybrids, greens that tasted bitter but stayed alive in heat. They filled their coats, bags, and pockets with seeds and roots, not because they remembered agriculture, but because their bodies recognized food.

Lioren's parents were among the agro‑engineers stationed at the test farm. Even with their cognition fractured, their hands remembered the land. They shouted instructions to the fleeing crowd—"Take the low‑growing roots, they store well," "Grab the red pods, they survive heat," "Fill your sleeves with the small seeds, they sprout fast"—not out of strategy, but out of instinct. They guided people toward the crops that would keep them alive, directing them away from the experimental strains that were toxic or unstable. In the chaos, their embodied knowledge became the difference between life and death.

The survivors fled toward the caves, driven by the primal need for shelter. The caves were cold, mineral‑rich, and dark—one of the few places where the climate's violence could not reach them. For years, people lived there, eating lentils, amaranth, millet, roots, and fungi scraped from the cave walls. They cooked over scavenged metal, boiled water in cracked containers, shaped flatbread from crushed seeds, and survived on foods that required no complex cognition to prepare. The engineered crops from the test farm became the foundation of their diet, the seed bank of a new world.

Lioren was three when the collapse happened. Too young for an implant, too young to understand why her parents sometimes stared at the cave walls as if waiting for them to speak. They could not remember Velhara, not truly. They could not remember the collapse or the escape. They could not remember the glass city or the mag‑lev lines or the life they had lived before the world broke. But they remembered how to make things grow. They remembered soil, and water, and the way a lentil root feels when it's ready to split. They remembered the land, even if they could not remember themselves.

Sometimes her father would have flashes—brief, disjointed images that struck him like lightning. A glass dome. A beeping console. A corridor lit in blue. Then darkness. He would freeze, eyes unfocused, breath held as if listening to something only he could hear. The last time it happened, he died staring at the ceiling of their cave, mouth open, as if he had finally touched upon some secret knowledge too large for the human mind. For hours, Lioren and her mother sat beside him, waiting for him to snort awake, wipe his bristly beard, and go out to tend the land as he always did. But he did not. That was the end.

Her mother carried on, quieter now, more brittle. She farmed because her hands remembered how. She cooked because her body remembered heat and water and hunger. She raised Lioren with the same instinctive care she gave the crops—steady, practical, without explanation. When the climate finally stabilized and people began returning to the ruins of Velhara, her mother refused. She had heard stories of violence in the reclaimed zones, of people fighting over salvage and territory, of minds still fractured by Corelink turning desperate and dangerous. She stayed where the land made sense, where her hands could still do what her mind could not. She raised Lioren in safety, far from the chaos of the resettled city.

When she died, rain ticking against the farmhouse roof, she gripped Lioren's wrist and whispered, "Go east." East had always meant weather, blight, and the road no one took unless the fields failed. But she said it again, as if a second time betrayed its meaning. "There are people there. Work, maybe. Shelter I think…if you can earn it." Then, softer: "I'm sorry, Li‑li." Her eyes moved over the ceiling as if reading a story written above them. "I think so…I'm sorry."

Those were her last words.

Lioren carried them east, through rain and dry wind and hunger, until she reached Velhara—a city being eaten by trees, where towers leaned like old bones and the market huddled beneath broken stone. She arrived wiry and weather‑toughened, wearing her mother's coat, carrying the ache of two parents who survived collapse not through memory, but through instinct. And now she stood beneath towers she did not understand, guided only by the land‑born knowledge her parents had given her, and the faint pulse of the codex at her wrist—a device pressed into her hand only five days earlier by Saori, a woman who had planted a field of questions that bore no answers, not yet.
